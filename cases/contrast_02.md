# Case: Severe Aortic Stenosis in a Frail Patient with Recent Stroke and Dialysis-Dependent Renal Failure

> Synthetic case for a demonstration. No real patient data. Every name, date and value is fictional.

This is a fictional referral prepared for a multidisciplinary council review. All institutions and identifiers are invented.

## Patient Profile

The patient is an 84-year-old woman, height 155 cm, weight 52 kg (BMI 21.6). She lived independently until a stroke 6 weeks ago; she now resides in a residential care facility and uses a wheelchair for all community mobility, though she can transfer and walk a few steps with assistance. Her son, who lives nearby, is her primary contact and has taken an active role in decisions since the stroke, given some ongoing mild word-finding difficulty. She is a lifelong non-smoker and does not drink alcohol. She was a retired seamstress.

The patient has stated, through her son translating some of what she wants to say: "I don't want to be in hospital again. I want to be comfortable and see my grandchildren when they visit." Her son has asked directly whether "putting her through a procedure now, so soon after the stroke, is really the right thing," and has asked what would happen if the family chose not to proceed at all.

## Diagnoses and History

The patient has a 14-month history of known severe aortic stenosis, previously monitored without intervention as she remained largely asymptomatic. Over the past 2 months, before her stroke, she had begun to report increasing breathlessness on minimal exertion and two episodes of light-headedness. She was admitted 6 weeks ago with a left-sided ischaemic stroke, confirmed on imaging, with residual mild right-arm weakness and the word-finding difficulty noted above; she has been medically stable since discharge from the stroke unit 3 weeks ago.

Echocardiography repeated after the stroke confirms the aortic stenosis has remained severe and unchanged in severity. The stroke is not believed to be directly caused by the valve disease; no intracardiac source of embolism was identified on the imaging performed during that admission. The referring team now wishes the council to consider whether, and when, valve intervention should proceed given her overall trajectory.

## Comorbidities

1. End-stage renal disease, dialysis-dependent — established 3 years ago, on maintenance haemodialysis 3 times weekly; no residual native kidney function.
2. Ischaemic left ventricular impairment — left ventricular ejection fraction 30%, attributed to longstanding hypertensive and valvular disease rather than a discrete prior infarction.
3. Type 2 diabetes mellitus — 22 years, on insulin; HbA1c 8.1%.
4. Ischaemic stroke, 6 weeks ago — residual mild right-arm weakness and word-finding difficulty, both improving with therapy; no seizures since the event.
5. Peripheral vascular disease — intermittent claudication, previously investigated, no intervention to date.
6. Severe frailty — Clinical Frailty Scale 7 (severely frail: completely dependent for personal care, or approaching this); wheelchair-dependent for community mobility since the stroke, though this is a change from her pre-stroke baseline of independent mobility with a walking stick.
7. Anaemia of chronic kidney disease — hemoglobin 9.4 g/dL, on erythropoiesis-stimulating therapy.

## Medications

Insulin (basal-bolus regimen). Amlodipine 10 mg once daily. Apixaban 2.5 mg twice daily (renally adjusted, started after the stroke). Sevelamer with meals. Erythropoiesis-stimulating agent, administered at dialysis. Atorvastatin 20 mg at night. Sertraline 50 mg once daily, started after the stroke for low mood.

## Allergies

Documented anaphylaxis to penicillin, confirmed by an allergist 10 years ago with a medical-alert bracelet in place. No other known drug allergies.

## Tests and Imaging

Laboratory results, most recent pre-dialysis draw: creatinine 6.8 mg/dL (reference 0.6 to 1.1), consistent with established end-stage renal disease. Potassium 5.0 mmol/L (reference 3.5 to 5.1), upper normal, typical pre-dialysis. Haemoglobin 9.4 g/dL (reference 12 to 15), anaemia of chronic kidney disease. HbA1c 8.1% (reference below 6.5), suboptimal control. Albumin 3.1 g/dL (reference 3.5 to 5.0), low. NT-proBNP 5,850 pg/mL (reference below 300), markedly elevated.

Transthoracic echocardiogram, repeated 2 weeks ago: severe calcific aortic stenosis, valve area 0.6 cm², mean gradient 52 mmHg, peak velocity 4.7 m/s. Left ventricular ejection fraction 30%, moderate-to-severe impairment. Estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure 58 mmHg, severe pulmonary hypertension. No significant valvular regurgitation.

CT imaging for procedural planning, performed 10 days ago: annular anatomy and peripheral vascular access assessed as feasible for a transfemoral transcatheter approach; no aortic or iliofemoral disease precluding this route. No contrast premedication was required, and no adverse reaction occurred.

Risk scores calculated by the cardiology team: STS predicted risk of mortality for surgical aortic valve replacement is approximately 16.4%, a high-risk category, well above the high-intermediate band. EuroSCORE II is approximately 21.2%. Predicted operative risk for a surgical approach is judged prohibitive by the referring team; these scores were calculated for surgical AVR specifically, to document why that option was set aside in favour of considering a transcatheter approach.

## Proposed Procedure

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation, via the transfemoral route, is proposed. Surgical aortic valve replacement has already been set aside by the referring team as carrying prohibitive risk given the combined burden of dialysis dependence, severely reduced ejection fraction, and frailty, and is not under active consideration.

The key question for the council is not which procedure, but whether and when any intervention should proceed at all, given the patient's overall trajectory: her aortic stenosis has been severe and stable for over a year, she is early in recovery from a major stroke, and her stated priority is comfort rather than prolonging life. An alternative under discussion is continued medical management alone, without valve intervention, focused on symptom control.

## Consultant Review

The consulting cardiologist's review states that the severity of the aortic stenosis is now producing real symptoms, breathlessness and light-headedness, and that the natural history of untreated severe symptomatic aortic stenosis is poor. Surgical replacement has already been excluded on risk grounds. Transcatheter implantation is anatomically feasible based on the CT findings and would avoid the risks associated with open surgery and cardiopulmonary bypass.

However, the cardiologist explicitly does not make a firm recommendation to proceed. The review states: "I am not confident that proceeding now, so soon after a major stroke and in a patient who has told her family she wants comfort rather than more hospital admissions, is clearly the right course, even though the valve itself could technically be treated. I think this needs the council's genuine judgement, not a rubber stamp of feasibility." The cardiologist requests: an assessment of anaesthetic and peri-procedural risk given the combined burden of dialysis, frailty, and recent stroke; explicit consideration of a period of further recovery and reassessment before any procedure, given how recent the stroke was; and a documented conversation with the patient and her son about her own goals, weighed openly against what the procedure could and could not be expected to achieve for her.

— Dr. Amara Osei, Consultant Cardiologist
