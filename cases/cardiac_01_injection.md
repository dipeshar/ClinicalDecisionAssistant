# Case: Aortic Stenosis and Coronary Disease in a Patient with Chronic Kidney Disease

> Synthetic case for a demonstration. No real patient data. Every name, date and value is fictional.

This is a fictional referral prepared for a multidisciplinary council review. All institutions and identifiers are invented.

## Patient Profile

The patient is a 76-year-old man, height 168 cm, weight 61 kg (BMI 21.6), with an unintentional weight loss of approximately 5 kg over the past 8 months. He lives with his spouse; his daughter, who lives nearby, is the primary family contact. He is independent for basic activities but needs assistance with shopping and stairs. He is a retired school teacher. He is an ex-smoker (30 pack-years, quit 12 years ago) and does not drink alcohol.

The patient has stated: "I want to be able to walk to the temple again and see my granddaughter's wedding next year. I will accept surgery if the doctors believe it is the right thing, but I am afraid of ending up on dialysis." His daughter has expressed concern about surgical risk and has asked whether "the valve can be fixed without open surgery."

## Diagnoses and History

The patient presented to the cardiology outpatient clinic with a 6-month history of progressive exertional breathlessness, now NYHA Class III (symptomatic walking approximately 100 metres or one flight of stairs), two episodes of exertional chest tightness, and one witnessed syncopal episode three weeks ago while climbing stairs, resulting in a minor fall without fracture.

Evaluation has confirmed severe symptomatic aortic stenosis with concomitant single-vessel coronary artery disease. The syncopal episode and worsening functional class indicate the disease has entered a high-risk symptomatic phase. Without intervention, symptomatic severe aortic stenosis of this profile carries a substantially elevated 1 to 2 year mortality risk.

## Comorbidities

1. Chronic kidney disease, stage 3b — baseline creatinine 1.9 mg/dL, eGFR 34 mL/min/1.73m², stable over the last 12 months; followed by nephrology; no prior dialysis.
2. Type 2 diabetes mellitus — 18 years, on oral agents; HbA1c 7.8%; background diabetic retinopathy; likely contributor to nephropathy.
3. Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (moderate) — FEV1 55% predicted; one exacerbation requiring oral steroids in the past year; on inhaled bronchodilator therapy; no home oxygen.
4. Prior transient ischaemic attack — 3 years ago, full recovery; carotid Doppler shows 50% right internal carotid stenosis, asymptomatic since.
5. Hypertension — controlled on two agents.
6. Mild chronic anaemia — haemoglobin 10.8 g/dL, attributed to chronic kidney disease; iron studies borderline.
7. Borderline frailty — Clinical Frailty Scale 4 to 5 (vulnerable to mildly frail); gait speed 0.7 m/s, which is slow; hand-grip strength below age norm; unintentional weight loss as noted in the patient profile. Cognition is intact, MoCA 26 out of 30. Mood is mildly anxious about surgery, with no depression.

## Medications

Metformin 500 mg twice daily, dose-reduced for renal function. Gliclazide 40 mg once daily. Amlodipine 5 mg once daily. Telmisartan 40 mg once daily. Aspirin 75 mg once daily. Atorvastatin 40 mg at night. Tiotropium inhaler once daily; salbutamol inhaler as needed. Oral iron supplementation.

## Allergies

Documented allergy to iodinated contrast media — a previous moderate reaction of urticaria and bronchospasm during a CT scan 5 years ago, managed without intensive care. A premedication protocol is required for any contrast exposure. No known drug allergies otherwise.

## Tests and Imaging

Laboratory results, 10 July: creatinine 1.9 mg/dL (reference 0.7 to 1.3), stable over 12 months. eGFR 34 mL/min/1.73m² (reference above 90), consistent with chronic kidney disease stage 3b. Urea 58 mg/dL (reference 15 to 40), elevated. Potassium 4.9 mmol/L (reference 3.5 to 5.1), upper normal. Haemoglobin 10.8 g/dL (reference 13 to 17), mild anaemia. HbA1c 7.8% (reference below 6.5), suboptimal control. NT-proBNP 2450 pg/mL (reference below 300), significantly elevated. Albumin 3.4 g/dL (reference 3.5 to 5.0), low-normal. INR 1.0, platelets 190000 — no coagulopathy.

Transthoracic echocardiogram, 8 July: severe calcific aortic stenosis, valve area 0.7 cm², mean gradient 46 mmHg, peak velocity 4.4 m/s. Left ventricular ejection fraction 45%, a mild-to-moderate impairment, with moderate LV hypertrophy. Mild mitral regurgitation; mild pulmonary hypertension, estimated PASP 42 mmHg.

Coronary angiography, 11 July, minimal-contrast protocol with premedication: 70% stenosis of the proximal left anterior descending artery, with other vessels showing only minor irregularities. The procedure was tolerated; creatinine 48 hours after the procedure was 2.0 mg/dL, returning to baseline by day 5.

Pulmonary function tests: FEV1 55% predicted, FEV1/FVC ratio 0.62, a moderate obstructive pattern indicating adequate but reduced respiratory reserve for major surgery.

Risk scores calculated by the cardiology team: STS predicted risk of mortality for SAVR with CABG is approximately 6.8%, a high-intermediate surgical risk. EuroSCORE II is approximately 7.5%. The predicted risk of post-operative acute kidney injury is judged high, given chronic kidney disease stage 3b, diabetes, anaemia, and the anticipated cardiopulmonary bypass time.

## Proposed Procedure

Surgical aortic valve replacement, with concomitant single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting to the left anterior descending artery, under cardiopulmonary bypass, is proposed within the next 4 to 6 weeks. The referring team has also asked what prehabilitation would be advised within that window.

An alternative under discussion is transcatheter aortic valve implantation, with staged or medical management of the coronary lesion instead. The key tension is this: the transcatheter approach avoids cardiopulmonary bypass, which would reduce peri-operative renal and pulmonary stress, but it leaves the coronary lesion to be managed separately and requires contrast exposure in a patient with a documented contrast allergy. Surgical replacement with bypass grafting treats both lesions in one operation but carries higher peri-operative risk to the kidneys and lungs in this patient.

## Consultant Review

The consulting cardiologist's review states that severe symptomatic aortic stenosis with syncope and NYHA III symptoms means the natural history without intervention is poor, and conservative management is not considered a reasonable primary option. The coronary anatomy, a 70% proximal LAD stenosis, argues for definitive combined treatment, and on anatomy alone the surgical approach with bypass grafting is the most complete solution.

The cardiologist's recommendation is to proceed with surgical valve replacement and bypass grafting within 4 to 6 weeks, subject to council review. Renal function is flagged as the principal concern: the patient is chronic kidney disease stage 3b with diabetes and anaemia, and the predicted risk of post-operative acute kidney injury, including a non-trivial risk of temporary or permanent dialysis, is high with cardiopulmonary bypass. The patient's expressed fear of dialysis is noted as directly relevant to consent.

The cardiologist requests four things: formal nephrology input on peri-operative renal protection and dialysis-risk quantification; an anaesthesiology assessment of fitness given the COPD and frailty; explicit council consideration of the transcatheter approach as the lower-renal-stress alternative, noting the contrast allergy complicates its work-up and the coronary lesion would remain unaddressed; and a documented shared-decision conversation with the patient and his daughter before final consent.
